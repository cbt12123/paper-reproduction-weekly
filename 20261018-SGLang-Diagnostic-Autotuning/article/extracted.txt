Benchmarking Ollama and vLLM for Concurrent LLM Serving:
A Multi-Scenario Evaluation of Performance and Scalability
Betül Ay *
 and Yunus Emre Demirda˘ g
Department of Computer Engineering, Fırat University, Elazı˘ g 23200, Türkiye; demirdag.emre.y@gmail.com
* Correspondence: betulay@firat.edu.tr
Abstract
Serving LLMs to many concurrent users gives rise to significant challenges for latency,
throughput, and scalability. This paper presents a systematic and reproducible benchmark
of two widely used large language model (LLM) serving frameworks, Ollama and vLLM,
under concurrent workloads. We conducted a controlled comparison by running both
frameworks on a single NVIDIA H100 80 GB GPU with the same model weights (Qwen3-
4B) and inference configurations. We then evaluated them using five open benchmark
datasets across four scenarios consisting of baseline question answering, complex reasoning,
streaming interaction, and stress testing. Each scenario was executed under increasing
concurrency levels, ranging from light loads to high-concurrency stress, to measure end-
to-end latency, throughput, time-to-first-token (TTFT), success rate, and resource usage.
Our experiments show that vLLM clearly outperforms Ollama across all four scenarios,
achieving a 100% request success rate. It delivers 20–29 times higher throughput and
8–19 times lower P95 latency, and it completes every request successfully. Additionally,
vLLM produced the first token within 0.5–3.5 s and remained stable at up to 100 concurrent
users. Ollama, by contrast, required 54–122 s for the first token, hit a concurrency bottleneck
near 10 users, and exhibited a timeout-based error rate of 13–30.06% under heavier loads.
Notably, both frameworks demonstrated significant memory growth during extended
endurance tests, necessitating careful monitoring in long-running deployments. Overall,
for a single model on a single GPU, vLLM is highly suitable for high-concurrency serving,
while Ollama remains a practical choice for lightweight, local, or developmental workflows.
Keywords: large language model serving; Ollama; vLLM; latency optimization; concurrent
request handling; continuous batching; FP8 quantization; PagedAttention; stress testing;
performance evaluation; Qwen3; NVIDIA H100
1. Introduction
LLMs have become one of the most important advances in artificial intelligence in
recent years. With models such as GPT, LLaMA, and Mistral, LLMs are now used in many
areas, including natural language understanding, text generation, question answering,
code synthesis, and decision-support systems [1]. Over time, LLMs have moved from being
mainly research tools to being practical solutions used in real production systems [2]. This
change has made the efficient, scalable, and low-latency deployment of LLMs an important
research and engineering problem [3].
Using LLMs in production brings several challenges. At first, these models needed
large hardware with high-memory GPUs and expensive clusters, so they were hard to use
for organizations with limited resources. To solve this, several techniques were developed

to reduce model size, improve parameter efficiency, and speed up inference. For example,
methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [4], model distillation [5], and quantiza-
tion [6] let LLMs run with fewer resources while keeping a reasonable balance between
accuracy and performance.
Even with these methods, latency and scalability under concurrent users are still major
problems in real systems [3,7]. For this reason, these problems led to the development of
special inference frameworks that aim to improve deployment efficiency. Frameworks such
as Hugging Face Transformers [8], Deepspeed-Inference [9], and TensorRT [10] try to solve
these issues. However, they often do not give consistent efficiency and scalability for large
production workloads.
In this context, two frameworks have recently received much attention: Ollama [11]
and vLLM [3]. Ollama is a lightweight framework that focuses on easy integration for
developers and efficient execution on local and edge devices. vLLM, on the other hand,
introduces techniques such as PagedAttention and optimized scheduling, which give
higher throughput and better use of hardware in large-scale serving. To the best of our
knowledge, there are still a few peer-reviewed comparisons of Ollama and vLLM under
controlled concurrent workloads. Thus, there is a need for systematic, multi-scenario
benchmarking of these frameworks under realistic concurrent load.
This study addresses this gap by systematically comparing Ollama and vLLM under
controlled, concurrent conditions on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU (NVIDIA Corporation,
Santa Clara, CA, USA) rented from the RunPod cloud platform (RunPod, Inc., Dover, DE,
USA; https://www.runpod.io/, accessed on 19 May 2026). We design four deployment
scenarios, namely baseline performance, complex reasoning, streaming interaction, and
stress testing, that simulate production use, and then we measure key metrics such as
end-to-end latency, throughput, TTFT, success rate, and resource usage. The results give
insights into the strengths and limits of each framework, and they offer practical guidance
for choosing a deployment strategy for different applications.
This article is organized as follows. Related work on efficient LLM inference and
serving frameworks is reviewed in Section 2. The experimental methodology, including the
hardware and software environment, the evaluation metrics, and the four test scenarios,
is elaborated in Section 3. The experimental results for all four scenarios, together with
the overall comparative analysis, are presented in Section 4. Section 5 presents a discus-
sion of the findings, the resource–performance trade-offs, and the causes of the observed
performance gap. Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.
2. Related Works
Recent studies show that the performance of LLMs in production depends strongly on
system-level optimizations, not only on model architecture [1–3]. In particular, memory
management, batching, and scheduling play a key role in latency and throughput [12,13].
In this section, we want to provide a general summary of the main methods proposed to
make LLM inference more efficient. The first group of methods targets the cost of a single
inference operation. For example, FlashAttention reduces memory use during attention
computation [14], while FlexGen enables high-throughput inference by offloading work
across CPU, GPU, and disk [ 15]. Quantization is also widely used to reduce computa-
tion and memory [6,16]. Among modern serving frameworks, vLLM stands out with its
PagedAttention mechanism, which improves KV-cache management and handles concur-
rent requests efficiently [3]. This approach greatly increases throughput and scalability,
especially under high load.
Other high-performance systems, such as Hugging Face TGI and TensorRT-LLM,
focus on optimized batching and hardware acceleration. Benchmarking studies show that
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16115435

these systems perform better than general-purpose runtimes under large workloads [17,18].
Ollama, by contrast, is designed for simplicity and easy deployment. It lets developers run
LLMs locally with little setup and provides a user-friendly interface [11]. However, its per-
formance under high-concurrency workloads has not been studied much in the literature.
A more recent line of work focuses on the scheduling layer of LLM inference. Yu
et al. [19] introduced iteration-level scheduling in ORCA, which lets requests enter and
leave a batch dynamically instead of waiting for the whole batch to finish. This idea,
now known as continuous batching, is the basis of most modern serving frameworks,
including vLLM. Building on this, Agrawal et al. [ 20] proposed Sarathi-Serve, which
combines chunked prefill with stall-free scheduling so that new requests do not interrupt
ongoing decodes. The chunked-prefill mechanism is also available in vLLM through the
enable-chunked-prefill flag and is part of our setup (Section 3.1.4).
Another direction separates the two phases of inference instead of merging them.
DistServe runs prefill and decode on different GPU pools, so each phase can be scaled on
its own [7], and Splitwise reports similar gains by splitting the phases across machines [21].
SGLang improves the throughput and latency of complex LM programs through new
optimizations [22] and reports large throughput gains over vLLM and TGI on workloads
with heavy prefix sharing. Together, these works show that modern serving frameworks
differ in basic design choices, which makes direct comparison important.
Some other methods speed up inference at the decoding step. Speculative decoding
uses a smaller model to draft several tokens at once, which the target model then checks
in a single step. Medusa removes the need for a separate draft model by adding several
decoding heads to the base model [23], while EAGLE performs speculation at the feature
level instead of the token level and reports higher acceptance rates than earlier methods [24].
These methods reduce per-token latency without changing the model weights, but their real
speedup depends on how the serving framework schedules drafted and verified tokens.
A separate line of work studies LLM inference outside the cloud. For example, Husom
et al. [25] evaluate 28 quantized models from the Ollama library on a Raspberry Pi 4 and
measure energy use, accuracy, and latency for different quantization levels. MELT is a
mobile benchmarking framework that runs LLMs on Android, iOS, and Jetson devices
and reports end-to-end latency, memory pressure, and energy use [ 26]. These studies
confirm that lightweight runtimes such as Ollama are useful on resource-limited hardware.
However, they do not study how the same runtimes behave on server-class hardware under
concurrent multi-user workloads, which is the setting we study in this paper.
Even though both frameworks are widely used, direct comparisons between Ollama
and vLLM are still limited. Most existing work studies either high-performance serving
systems or local deployment tools separately [27,28]. Some prior work benchmarks local
LLM runtimes on specific hardware, such as Apple Silicon [ 28], but does not directly
compare local deployment frameworks with high-performance serving systems such as
vLLM. Therefore, a unified evaluation that compares these frameworks under the same
conditions is needed. We fill this gap with a unified evaluation that compares the two
frameworks under identical conditions across several scenarios, namely baseline, reasoning,
streaming, and stress, which are largely unexplored in the literature.
3. Methodology
3.1. Experimental Environment
To make the comparison of Ollama and vLLM fair and reproducible, we ran all experi-
ments on a high-performance server. Here we provide the hardware and software details.

3.1.1. Hardware Configuration
The system uses an Intel Xeon Platinum 8470 processor (Intel Corporation, Santa Clara,
CA, USA), which is an enterprise-class CPU. During idle benchmarking, the average CPU
load was about 9%. The system has 233.8 GiB of DDR5 RAM, and only 3.533 GiB was used
at baseline. This confirms that background processes had little effect on the experiments.
For inference, we used a single NVIDIA H100 80 GB HBM3 GPU (NVIDIA Corpora-
tion, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The GPU has 80 GiB of HBM3 memory, supports a maximum
power draw of 700 W, and runs at the P0 performance state. Figure 1 presents the GPU
status, obtained with NVIDIA-SMI. As Figure 1 confirms, the GPU was idle with 0% utiliza-
tion and almost no memory use at the time of capture, which confirms a clean baseline. The
storage consists of a 100 GB system disk (4% used) and a 250 GB data volume (31 GB, 13%
used). This is enough space for model weights, Docker images, and experiment outputs.
Figure 1. GPU information of the test environment, obtained via NVIDIA-SMI.
3.1.2. Software Environment
The server runs Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS (Noble) with Python 3.11 as the main program-
ming language. We used Docker 29.2.1 for containerized deployment, which ensures
reproducibility and isolation between experiments. The GPU software stack includes
NVIDIA driver version 580.126.09 and CUDA Toolkit 13.0. We used Ollama version 0.24.0
and vLLM version 0.17.0 as the inference frameworks under evaluation. In addition, we
used Hugging Face Transformers 4.57.6 and huggingface_hub 0.36.2 for model and dataset
access, and psutil 7.2.2 and GPUtil 1.4.0 for system-resource monitoring.
3.1.3. Preparation of the Test Environment
To make the experiments objective and repeatable, we applied a fixed pre-run proce-
dure before each experiment. First, we prefetched all Hugging Face model weights and
datasets into a local cache using a dedicated script. This removed network variability
during inference. A preflight script then checked the GPU compute capability and automat-
ically selected the right quantization path for vLLM: FP8 native precision on Hopper-class
GPUs (compute capability ≥ 8.9) and GPTQ on older hardware. On the H100 used here, the
FP8 native path was selected. This yields an approximately 8-bit weight-equivalent setup,
comparable to Ollama’s Q8_0 GGUF format, so the quantization baseline is fair between
the two frameworks.
Only one framework was active at any time. Between framework changes, we applied
a 60 s cooldown, optionally followed by clearing the Linux page cache. Between scenario
changes within the same framework, we applied a 15 s cooldown. Each scenario had a 30 s
warm-up phase before measurement and a 30–120 s cooldown after it. We excluded all
warm-up and cooldown measurements from the reported results.
We checked the GPU temperature before each scenario with a thermal-check script
and logged the readings to a file. If the temperature went above 70 ◦C, we held for 60 s
before starting measurements. During the recorded run, the GPU temperature stayed in
the 30–32 ◦C range in all scenarios, which confirms there was no thermal throttling.

3.2. Evaluation Metrics
Here we provide one common set of metrics collected across all four scenarios to
evaluate Ollama and vLLM under different conditions. We measured all metrics from the
client side using a shared HTTP streaming driver, so the measurements are consistent and
comparable between the two frameworks. The metrics are summarized in Table 3. A wide
range of metrics was collected to evaluate Ollama and vLLM across different deployment
scenarios. These metrics were grouped into categories reflecting fundamental performance,
streaming, token usage, reasoning quality, system resources and scalability.
Core performance metrics capture the basic behavior of the system. They include
end-to-end latency, throughput, TTFT, token generation rate, and error rate. End-to-end
latency is the wall-clock time from the moment the HTTP request is sent to the moment the
full response is received. It includes client overhead, network round-trip time, server-side
queuing, prefill, and decode. We do not instrument these parts separately, so all latency
values are client-observed end-to-end measurements. TTFT is the time from the request to
the first non-empty token in streaming mode, and we report it for all four scenarios. We
compute latency and TTFT only from successful requests. We exclude failed or timed-out
requests from the average and percentile values, but we count them in the error rate.

Metric Definitions and Measurement Methodology
To keep the metrics clear and consistent, we define all of them from one client-side
point of view. Here, the life cycle of a request is shown in Figure 2.
Figure 2. Conceptual request timeline used for metric definitions.
In this timeline, t0 is the moment the client sends the streaming HTTP request, t3 is
the moment the first non-empty token is received, and t4 is the moment the full streamed
response is finished. The middle phases (network transmission, server-side queuing, and
inference, that is, prefill and decode) are shown only conceptually. The measurement har-
ness does not instrument these phases separately. For this reason, all reported latency and
TTFT values are client-observed end-to-end measurements that include all of these parts.
End-to-end latency is measured as t4 − t0: the wall-clock time from the request to the
final token. In Scenario 1, we report this as latency. In Scenarios 2, 3, and 4, we report it as
completion time. Both refer to the same client-observed measurement.
Time-to-first-token (TTFT) is measured as t3 − t0: the time from the request to the
first non-empty token. All four scenarios use streaming, so we report TTFT for all of them.
TTFT includes network overhead, server-side scheduling delay, and prefill. It reflects
client-perceived responsiveness, not isolated model inference time.

We send all requests over HTTP streaming. The timer origin (t0) is recorded just before
the streaming request is sent in the client driver. We compute latency and TTFT averages
only from successful requests. We count failed and timed-out requests in the error rate but
exclude them from latency, TTFT, and token-rate values.
Throughput is the number of successful requests divided by the total test duration,
and it should be read together with the error rate. The token generation rate is the number
of completion tokens divided by the end-to-end time per request.
Quality-oriented metrics (reasoning quality in Scenario 2, and response coherence,
response quality, and context retention in Scenarios 3 and 4) use lightweight heuristic
scoring. These scores do not use human annotation or LLM-as-judge evaluation. They
should be read as approximate proxy indicators of output quality under load, not as
validated quality measurements.
The measurement harness does not separately instrument server-side queue waiting
time and pure inference time. Therefore, we make no claims about how the observed
latency splits into queuing and inference. All reported values are the combined client-
observed measurement.
3.3. Statistical Analysis
We ran each scenario as a single continuous run at each concurrency level and aggre-
gated the per-request statistics over the full test duration. In addition to average values, we
also report percentile distributions (P50, P75, P90, P95, and P99), minimum and maximum
values, and variability-aware plots to make the results easier to read.
We analyzed latency and TTFT with percentile-based statistics, because inference
latency under concurrent load is not Gaussian. This follows common practice in tail-latency
analysis of large-scale serving systems [36]. We also use error bars and range plots where
useful to show request-level variability.
We exclude failed and timed-out requests from the latency and TTFT averages but
count them in the error rate. The harness does not instrument server-side queue time and
inference time separately, so all reported latency values are combined client-observed end-
to-end measurements, as defined in the Metric Definitions and Measurement Methodology.
Several distributions are heavily skewed and unstable under overload, especially for
Ollama at high concurrency. For this reason, our analysis is mainly descriptive rather than
based on formal parametric significance tests.
3.4. Test Scenarios
To study the behavior of Ollama and vLLM under different conditions, we designed
four scenarios. Each one focuses on a different aspect of deployment: baseline performance,
complex reasoning, streaming quality, and stress resilience. All scenarios use the same
HTTP streaming client path, the same sampling parameters, and a single GPU. Here, the
only difference between the frameworks is the inference backend itself.

In this scenario, we measure the basic performance of both frameworks on short
factual question-answering tasks from the TriviaQA dataset [ 31]. It focuses on latency,
throughput, and error rates under different concurrency levels.
• Model: Qwen3-4B.
• Concurrency: 10, 50, 100 requests.
• Duration: 180 s per level.
• Warm-up: 30 s (excluded from measurements).

3.4.3. Scenario 3: Streaming and Conversational
In this scenario, we study streaming responsiveness and conversation quality. It uses
multi-turn dialogs from the MT-Bench [35] prompt set. We evaluate both frameworks on
their ability to deliver tokens in real time and to keep context across conversation turns.
• Model: Qwen3-4B.
• Concurrency: 50–150 conversations.
• Duration: 900 s per level.

5. Discussion
5.1. Which Framework Is More Suitable in Different Scenarios?
The results demonstrate that vLLM delivers better scalability and reliability in all four
scenarios. For this reason, it is the better choice for production systems with concurrent
serving, streaming, or stress-heavy demands. Its throughput of 9.9–13.3 req/s across scenar-
ios and its predictable latency below 10 s at up to 100 users give a stable user experience in
real-time systems such as chatbots, customer support assistants, and interactive reasoning
agents. Its 100% success rate in all scenarios also makes it suitable for production, where
request reliability is important.
Ollama, by contrast, is not competitive under concurrent load, but it is still useful for
lightweight, local, or single-user deployments. On the test hardware, its effective limit
is about 10 users, beyond which throughput stagnates and errors rise. Its simple setup,
GGUF model format, and low operational overhead make it suitable for personal assistants,
developer prototyping, edge deployment, and settings with little operational complexity.
The heuristic quality scores on completed requests (coherence 0.74–0.76, response quality
0.63) indicate that Ollama’s output quality on finished requests is similar to vLLM’s. This
means that its limits come from the serving architecture, not from the model. Hence, Ollama
fits small-scale or offline use, while vLLM fits high-concurrency serving much better.
5.2. Resource vs. Performance Trade-Off
The two frameworks turn hardware into serving capacity in different ways. Ollama
used slightly more CPU (9–10%) than vLLM (4–6%) across scenarios, but it used less GPU
(15–17% vs. 35–69%). This indicates that Ollama underuses the H100, while vLLM gets
much more inference work per GPU cycle through continuous batching, PagedAttention,
and FP8 native execution. FP8 generation on Hopper-class GPUs has independently been
reported to deliver substantial decode-throughput gains over BF16 at comparable accuracy,
which is consistent with the higher GPU efficiency we observe for vLLM [41]. This pattern
is not specific to our framework or hardware: independent studies evaluating vLLM
against a different competitor (Hugging Face TGI), a different model family (LLaMA-2),
and different hardware (A100) likewise report that vLLM attains substantially higher
throughput and GPU utilization through continuous batching and PagedAttention, while
the weaker framework saturates at lower concurrency [ 17]. This is consistent with our
observations relative to Ollama. As a result, vLLM delivers 20–29 times higher throughput
and 8–19 times lower P95 latency while using similar or less CPU.
One notable point is the memory growth during endurance testing. Both frameworks
grew over the 3600 s window (Ollama: about 5602 MB; vLLM: about 14,219 MB), but
vLLM’s higher growth happened while it processed about 33 times more requests. Per
request, vLLM’s memory footprint during endurance testing is much lower, which fits
its FP8 KV-cache strategy. Still, the absolute growth in vLLM should be monitored in
long-running use. This aligns with prior work showing that memory pressure and frag-
mentation under sustained, high-concurrency serving can degrade scalability, motivating
dynamic scheduling and migration techniques [42]. This trade-off shows that the choice of
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16115435
===== PAGE 26 =====
Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 5435 26 of 30
framework should match the hardware and the workload: Ollama for resource-limited but
low-demand settings, and vLLM for performance-driven production systems with enough
GPU memory and monitoring.
5.3. Implications for Deployment Scenarios
From a deployment point of view, the results are clear. vLLM keeps a 100% success
rate and predictable latency under concurrent loads up to 100 users, so it is a strong choice
for high-concurrency serving under the tested conditions. It supports auto-scaling, load
balancing, and fault-tolerant deployment, which are important for large-scale reliability. Its
degradation beyond 100 users is gradual latency increase rather than failure, which gives
operators a clear warning sign for capacity planning. Memory use should be monitored to
handle long-term growth.
Ollama’s limits in scalability and stability, in particular its 30.06% error rate in stream-
ing and 13–13.3% error rates in baseline and reasoning at 100 users, restrict it to lightweight
or single-user settings. Its simplicity, GGUF model format, and local-first design make it
useful where easy integration and developer access matter more than raw performance,
such as on-device inference, developer prototyping, or constrained-resource settings. In
the end, the choice between Ollama and vLLM should depend on the target environment:
vLLM for high-throughput, latency-sensitive serving with concurrent users, and Ollama for
lightweight, resource-limited, or edge settings where concurrent serving is not a main need.
5.4. Diagnostic Analysis of Performance Degradation
We observe that the strong degradation of Ollama under concurrent load seems to
come from internal queue saturation, limited parallel request handling, and the lack of
continuous batching at the inference level. Its effective concurrency limit (10 users in
the stress test) is close to its set number of parallel slots, which suggests that the bottle-
neck is structural, not hardware-bound. Matching such parallelism configurations across
frameworks is a recognized baseline-fairness concern when evaluating inference serving
systems [43]. As concurrency rose past this limit, request queues filled quickly, response
times became dominated by queue waiting, and timeout failures appeared at 100 users
in baseline and reasoning (39–40% error rate) and at 150 conversations in streaming (61%
error rate).
vLLM, by contrast, kept stable scheduling and continuous batching under the same
load, with a 100% success rate at all levels. This strongly suggests that batching strategy,
KV-cache management, and request scheduling are the main factors for scalable infer-
ence, which agrees with prior work on iteration-level scheduling [19] and chunked-prefill
scheduling [20]. The much higher TTFT for Ollama (54–122 s P95 across scenarios, vs.
0.5–3.5 s for vLLM) shows that requests waited a long time before token generation started.
Since we measured latency from the client side, these values include queue buildup and
serialized inference, not just model compute time.
Ollama’s degradation followed a structural overload pattern, not a sudden failure.
As concurrency rose, request queues filled quickly, average completion time rose sharply
(from 21 s at 10 users to 100 s at 100 users in baseline), and some requests began to time
out. However, throughput stayed flat at about 0.45 req/s at all levels, which shows that the
framework could not turn extra load into serving capacity beyond its parallel-slot limit.
5.5. Limitations
This study has some limitations to keep in mind. First, we used a single model family
(Qwen3-4B) on one hardware setup with a single NVIDIA H100 80 GB GPU. Therefore, the
results may not transfer directly to other model architectures, parameter sizes, quantization
formats, or hardware. In particular, the FP8 native path for vLLM is available only on
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16115435
===== PAGE 27 =====
Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 5435 27 of 30
Hopper-class and newer GPUs. On older hardware, vLLM would fall back to GPTQ or
FP16 execution, which may narrow the gap. Ollama’s relative performance may also differ
on hardware where its GGUF path is more competitive.
Second, we drew the scenarios from five open Hugging Face datasets (TriviaQA,
GSM8K, HumanEval, HotpotQA, MT-Bench) under controlled concurrency, not from fully
mixed real-world traffic. The scenarios cover question answering, multi-step reasoning,
streaming dialog, and stress workloads, but real systems may have more complex request
mixes, more workload variety, and multi-tenant behavior than we captured.
Third, several quality metrics (reasoning accuracy, response coherence, response qual-
ity, and context retention) use lightweight heuristic methods, rather than human annotation,
embedding-based metrics such as BERTScore [44], or LLM-as-judge evaluation. The 0.00 ac-
curacy for Ollama in Scenario 2 comes from timeout-induced truncation and heuristic-
scoring limits, not from purely wrong answers. Therefore, this metric should be read as a
proxy for the quality of completed output under load, not as an exact quality measure.
Fourth, the measurement harness does not separately instrument server-side queue
waiting time and pure inference time. All latency and TTFT values are combined client-
observed measurements that include network overhead, queuing, prefill, and decode. This
keeps the two frameworks consistent, but it limits how finely we can attribute latency. Finer
internal traces would be needed to fully split queuing from inference under overload.
Finally, we focused on single-node, single-GPU deployments. We did not study
distributed inference, multi-node scaling, energy efficiency, or cost–performance trade-offs.
We reported the endurance memory growth as cumulative resource-monitor readings, not
steady-state leak rates, and a longer multi-day test would be needed to confirm long-term
stability. Future work should extend the study to broader settings, larger models, and
longer endurance windows to check how well these findings generalize.
6. Conclusions and Future Work
This paper presents a systematic benchmark of Ollama and vLLM across four scenarios:
baseline performance, complex reasoning, streaming, and stress testing. We construct the
comparison on a single NVIDIA H100 80 GB GPU using the same Qwen3-4B model weights,
the same sampling parameters, and deterministic subsamples from five open benchmark
datasets (TriviaQA, GSM8K, HumanEval, HotpotQA, MT-Bench). This setup ensures that
the only differences come from the internal design of the two frameworks.
Our experiments demonstrate that vLLM delivers higher throughput (20–29 times
higher across scenarios), much lower latency (8–19 times lower P95 latency), and near-
perfect reliability (a 100% success rate in all scenarios) under concurrent load. We demon-
strate that it remains stable up to about 100 users before its degradation zone at 200 users,
while still completing all requests. Its FP8 native execution on Hopper hardware, com-
bined with continuous batching and PagedAttention-based KV-cache management, yields
a decode throughput of over 1800 tokens per second in baseline and over 3200 tokens per
second in reasoning.
Ollama, by contrast, reaches a structural concurrency limit at about 10 users on the
test hardware, with throughput flat at 0.45 req/s regardless of concurrency. Beyond this
limit, queue saturation causes request timeouts that range from 13% in baseline to over
30% in streaming. We find that the heuristic quality scores on completed Ollama requests
(coherence 0.74–0.76, response quality 0.63) are close to those of vLLM, which indicates that
the gap comes mainly from the serving architecture rather than from generation quality.
During endurance testing, both frameworks exhibit notable resource growth over the
3600 s window (about 5.6 GB for Ollama and about 14.2 GB for vLLM), although vLLM
processes roughly 33 times more requests in the same window. Per request, vLLM’s memory
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16115435
===== PAGE 28 =====
Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 5435 28 of 30
footprint is therefore much lower, but the absolute growth should still be monitored in
long-running use.
Beyond the head-to-head comparison, this work contributes a unified four-scenario
pipeline (baseline, reasoning, streaming, and stress testing) and an open benchmarking
framework that lets others reproduce the same comparison with other models, frameworks,
and hardware. The pipeline provides deterministic dataset pinning through Hugging Face
commit SHAs, structured environment logging, unified streaming HTTP measurement,
and modular post-processing for paper-ready tables and charts. With this work, we
highlight that vLLM is the more robust choice for scalable LLM inference under concurrent
load on Hopper-class hardware, while Ollama remains useful for lightweight, local, or
developer deployments.
There is still room for future work in this direction. In particular, the comparison
could be extended to larger models and multi-node deployments, and the memory growth
observed during endurance testing could be studied over longer runs to confirm long-term
stability. Another useful direction is to add energy and cost-performance analysis. In this
work, we measured only performance and resource usage; future studies could additionally
report metrics such as tokens per watt, energy use per request, and cost per request, thereby
providing a more complete basis for deployment decisions. Finally, the workloads here
were controlled and separated into distinct scenarios. Real systems often serve mixed and
bursty traffic with many request types at once, as well as multi-tenant usage. Future work
could therefore test both frameworks under mixed, realistic workloads that better reflect
production traffic.
